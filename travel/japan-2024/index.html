Notes from Japan

Tokyo & Hakone

TL;DR
Highly recommend Japan as a place to visit. Hope to revisit multiple times and actually even dream of living in Japan for at least a few years some time in the future.

Expect it to be harder than most developed countries to find your way around the metro - pause, take a breath and figure out the metro maps yourself - adds to the experience. And the local food palette may be quite different from what you’re used to.

Tips

  • Use this to expedite immigration process at airport
  • Take the Narita express from Narita airport to Tokyo central
  • Carry some Yen from home country and enable international transactions on your credit card
  • Explore Tokyo by walk and public transport as much as possible.
  • Expect the Tokyo metro to be complex. Look for the info desks to ask for help.
  • Plenty of convenience stores located all over the place with precooked food options

Context

I have the utmost respect and admiration for Japan, its people and the Japanese culture. Some of my raw observations below may come across negative, but they may reflect my own shortcomings more than objective truth about the people in Tokyo. I’m just trying to share my honest observations - regardless of whether they seem positive or negative.

Infrastructure and efficiency

  • Japan is super well organized, clean, efficient and infra is truly next level. Unable to understand how they could be at this state in 2024 despite being basically economically stagnant for 2-3 decades before our visit. Japan’s GDP CAGR from 1990 to 2024 is 0.82%.
    • They went from losing WW2, hit by two atom bombs, to launching bullet trains in 1964. 1964! That’s 60 years before my visit. That’s despite being the capital of the “ring of fire” and having to deal with earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanoes. India’s 1st bullet train project was announced in 2015, expected to be live by 2022 but now delayed to 2029. Japan’s 1st corridor went live in ~5.5 years.
  • Everything was on time, always. 1 min late = next train.
  • We decided to take the Shinkansen (bullet train) back from Odawara to Tokyo. When waiting at the station, we experienced 3-4 flybys. It was crazy! The one we took hit 290kmph and the overall experience excellent - super clean, super steady, massive and clean bathrooms, all in all much better than a plane.
  • Ochanomizu is a beautiful spot to see three train lines cross each other.

Getting around

  • We really enjoyed walking around and exploring Tokyo. By far the most walkable and accessibility friendly city we’ve been to. Door to door, anywhere to anywhere with a stroller, no problems.
  • Language - ~5%-10% ppl spoke some basic English, rest only Japanese.
  • Convenience stores were everywhere, with pre-prepared food that seemed high quality.
  • Digital payments was interestingly far behind India. Ppl still use cash/coins a lot!
  • The sun rises at 4:40am, everyone starts their day early. Everything shuts by 10pm.

Clean, neat and safe

  • No dust or loose soil anywhere, literally did not see mud anywhere in Tokyo
  • Even clothes that were hung out to dry seemed super organized and neat. Cars/vehicles were parked perfectly outside homes.
  • Everyone was dressed prim and proper, mostly in western formal basics. Whites, blacks, navy blue mostly.
  • Felt very safe, even dark alleyways, shady looking places. Everyone seemed like working folks on their way somewhere.
  • Toilets were exceptionally clean everywhere, even in the metro stations that were very old. Most of them were also quite spacious. This was super helpful for parents traveling with a young child. What’s more - the electronic bidet is a lifesaver for those of us born and bred in India. IYKYK.
  • Saw <5 homeless people despite walking across Tokyo for ~4 days. Of the places we’ve visited, only Singapore was lower I guess.

People and culture

  • Japan has a very strong and unique culture - food, language, lifestyle, pop culture etc that seems to be quite unique and different from what you’d expect in the western world.
  • Most people seemed to be serious and in a hurry, walking fast to wherever they were going.
  • It was surprising to see so many young women/girls standing around in public places waiting to be “approached”. We saw lots of old men walking around with much younger women, with some definite “hanky-panky” happening. We even saw some men approaching/trying to catcall women in some of the quieter streets, but the men seemed to respect ’no’. (Ref: link, link)
  • Also, a LOT of people seem to eat out and drink every day. One interesting observation, the % of single seating in Tokyo restaurants seemed extremely high. As per gemini its ~30-50% in Tokyo vs ~10-20% USA vs <5% India.
  • Extremely few kids, at least around where we visited and stayed
  • Ppl take their jobs very very seriously, we found help late in the night from a guy who was manning a crossing at a construction site .. he was actually there doing his job. In India the guy would have been asleep. This seems to be the norm, across all strata of society. We saw a janitor in the metro station cleaning the escalator with utmost dedication. It was beautiful and inspiring.
  • What I am sharing next is honestly what I felt at the moment. After further reflection, I feel like it was me who was in the wrong most of the time. Tokyo can be a little “uncomfortable” for people that are used to getting help from people around them (eg: Indians). I felt the people in Tokyo came across a bit cold and unhelpful. Example - Strangers on the station were generally unwilling to help guide us as tourists, one of them was kind enough to direct us to an “info” desk though (which we did not know existed). They were quite strict about following SOPs and rules in general. Eg: There were a couple of times at the hotel where we reached just as the serving hours were shutting down and requested some leeway for our 2yo but were flatly refused, which is fine. I realise that the Indian mentality of “rules are just guidelines” is what caused this minor culture shock. What could have been better is if the hotel staff simply advised us that we could find food in a nearby convenience store. It was our first day, we were not aware. In similar situations in the US, people did deny but they also helped suggest an alternate solution - this just makes tourists feel more welcome I guess. But I did notice that people outside of Tokyo seemed to be more warm than central Tokyo areas. Objectively, this was on us. We were unaware and possibly did not have enough exposure to different cultures.

Food and water

  • Felt quite capitalist overall. Packaged drinking water was not easy to come by, similar to other developed countries. But in the western countries, it was possible to buy packs of multiple smaller bottles at a discounted price. In Tokyo, we couldn’t find these and found only larger bottle sizes. Since we were tourists, we were not comfortable drinking tap water (would not be immunised to the local bacteria, thats the common belief at least)
  • Food palette was very different from what I was used to - I personally didn’t enjoy it at all! Everything felt like it was cooked in some kind of fish oil (on further reading I learned about dashi). Most restaurants had lots of fish, beef, pork but seemingly no veggies/fruits. In fact we couldn’t even find fresh fruit/veggies in the convenience stores around town. I’m sure there are some local details we missed. Maybe it’s because we primarily visited the central parts of Tokyo. Now, I usually judge people who travel to a new place and still go to their native restaurants and eat the food they are used to. But by god, after trying 3-4 Japanese restaurants, and both me and my son going hungry for 2 days, we went to an Indian restaurant for dinner on the 2nd night. By god! it felt like the best North Indian food I’ve had in my life - Dal Makhani, Butter Chicken, Naan, Khichdi for the toddler. The cooks were mostly Nepali.
  • We found ourselves wandering the streets of “Marunouchi” at midnight with a toddler in the stroller looking for food. We ended up getting a few massive bananas and some yummy cup noodles.

Tokyo Zoo

  • Tokyo Zoo was a beautiful experience, well managed, lots of animals and the glass wall experience was very nice. Our 2yo loved it, we loved it. He especially loved the Ezo Brown bear, which he called “Bown Bae” for a long time. Having said that, I must say that India possibly has some of the best wildlife diversity in the world - Lions, Tigers, Leopards, Elephants, Gaur, Rhinos, Camel, Wolves. Visited Mysore and Vizag zoos recently and both were actually very well run and were light years ahead of the Zoo’s I remember seeing ~20 years back in India.

Hakone

  • We took the “Romance Car” from Tokyo city to Hakone. Do not recommend it, nothing too romantic and quite expensive. All trains in Tokyo are beautiful. Just take any train.
  • Fresh Japanese mountain air/breeze in the pirate ship ride across lake Ashi - amazing
  • Riding a cable car over an active volcano - amazing. They sell eggs boiled in the volcanic soil, which are believed to increase your lifespan by 7 years.
  • The onsen was a very unique and magical experience. I was extremely uncomfortable going naked, but luckily - no one else was around when I went. From what I understood, it is supposed to be a sacred experience and hence one must shed their clothes and shower before getting into the pool. Skin felt cleansed and super fresh for multiple weeks after. (Side note: You get a small hand towel to take with you into the premises. You are free to use it as you please.)
  • Japanese engineering and efficiency is really next level. The hakone cable car was first built in 1921 and still ran reliably. Interestingly some of these trains were manufactured by Kawasaki. I had thought Kawasaki only made motorcycles.

Side notes to self

  • You can make things happen. But you need to make a decision and act. Preferably fast. At least, this is what works for me. The whole Japan trip was a spur of the moment thing, idea to execution was like one month. I was dreaming of going to Japan without acting on it for many years.
  • If this is what a developed country looks like, just can’t imagine how India will bridge such a massive gap. Having said that, ~ 30yrs back, we couldn’t afford international trips. We didn’t even consider it. Now we can afford 1 every year - combination of India growing and us climbing the ladder a little thanks to education sponsored by our parent’s sacrifices.
  • Our corporate jobs, as much as we complain about them, have given us significant privileges in the world. Even at a global level, it feels like we are at or near the median level. We could afford stuff an avg American/European could. Did not feel poor. Touch wood.
